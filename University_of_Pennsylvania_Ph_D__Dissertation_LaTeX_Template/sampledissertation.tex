\subsection{Formation Mechanisms}
\subsubsection{Core Accretion}
The widely-accepted model for core accretion (CA hereon) comes from Pollack et al. 1996. A general overview of the process is as follows: a protoplanetary disk consists of dust and gas. This dust undergoes random motion, and eventually dust particles may collide and clump together. The repetition of this process, named planetesimal accretion, eventually leads to pebbles, then boulders, plentesimals, then a planetary core massive enough to start accreting gas from the disk, which eventually leads to runaway gas accretion (citation). All in all, this process' timescale is on the scale of Myr, which is significant because if a planet is found to be significantly younger than its host star, and its separation to its host star is small ($\leq$50 AU), it is possible the planet was formed through CA.

This process occurs closer to the centre of the disk than gravitational instability, the other widely-accepted formation pathway, detailed in the next section. Note that this process is not necessarily linear; most pebbles actually don't evolve to the metre-scale. Among the potential barriers include the fragmentation-barrier, scattering-barrier and others that are beyond the scope of this project but are detailed in Drazkowska et al. 2023.

There were problems with older models of planet formation. Pollack et al. 1996 highlights previous developments that addressed the following:
\begin{itemize}
\item They couldn't explain why the giant planets had similar high-Z masses (high atomic number) and core masses 
\item They couldn't explain the high-presence of high-Z materials in the atmospheres of the giants
\end{itemize}

Mizuno et al. 1978 and Mizuno 1980 proposed the "core instability" (CI hereon) model based on the giant planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune). The details of the model are not significant (but can be found in Mizuno et al. 1980) but they showed low-Z mass envelopes (accreted gas phase) grew exponentially with increasing core mass (contains high-Z materials, formed from planetismal accretion), 
with the low-Z mass eventually equalling the high-Z mass when the core reaches a certain critical mass; Mizuno reports $M_{crit} \sim 10M_\oplus$, which would correspond to the end of the planetesimal accretion phase. Gas accretion would occur soon hereafter. Mizuno's models' critical core mass threshold was insensitive to varying distance, which explained why the high-Z masses (core masses) of the giants were similar and on the scale of $10M_\oplus$ to $30_\oplus$.

Bodenheimer and Pollack 1986 (BP86) used different evolutionary tracks of Mizuno's models and found planetesimal accretion is actually time-invariant for a given track. They did vary the accretion rate for different tracks though. BP86 reports that the rate of gas accretion exceeds planetesimal accretion at some mass they called critical mass, corresponding to Mizuno's critical mass. Pollack 1996 calls it "crossover mass" $M_{cross}$. They report the same distance-insensitivity as Mizuno but find that $M_{cross}$ is a bit higher, at $10M_\oplus$ to $30M_\oplus$. $M_{cross}$ is essentially the harbinger of runaway gas accretion.

Pollack et al. 1986 and Podolack et al. 1988 examined planetesimal accretion, specifically how massive the envelope of the forming planet must be before planetesimals stop contributing to the core mass, and is instead shorn apart by the gravtiational force of the planetary core and dissolves into the outer envelope. They find this happens when the envelope mass is a few percent of $M_\oplus$. This dissolution enriches the high-Z content of the envelope (since planetesimals are respnsible for the high-Z mass in the core).

However, this model couldn't explain why Uranus and Neptune's low-Z mass was only about 10\%-20\% the high-Z mass; this implies the planets wouldn't have had enough mass to reach $M_{cross}$, yet their core masses are similar to Jupiter and Saturn. Pollack et al. 1996's model aims to remedy tihs.

\section{Margins}
\textbf{As of January 2023, the margins must be 1 inch on each side.} Remember to resolve all overfull or underfull warnings to help ensure your dissertation meets the margin requirements.\footnote{This is a common source of errors -- please double check the margins before submitting your final dissertation.} Click \underline{\href{https://www.overleaf.com/learn/how-to/Understanding_underfull_and_overfull_box_warnings\#How_the_Error_Happens}{here}} to learn how to identify and resolve the warnings. Common causes of warnings include 
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{LaTeX CANNOT automatically break long mathematical expressions or parenthetical references across multiple lines.} Chapter~\ref{ch:equations} demonstrates two methods for manually splitting equations.
\item You can manually control the hyphenation by using the \Verb|\hyphenation{}| hints command. For example, write \Verb|\hyphenation{rep-re-sent-a-tive}| in the preamble to tell LaTeX where it can break the word ``representative.''
\item The Verbatim environment and the \Verb|\Verb| command remove LaTeX's formatting controls. There may be instances that require you to manually adjust line breaks.
\item The breaklinks option of the hyperref package is activated, however there may be instances of URLs running into the margins that require your manual intervention.
\end{itemize}

\section{Template Tips}
\subsection{Chapters, sections, and subsections}
Create chapters with the \Verb|\chapter| command, sections with the \Verb|\section| command, and subsections with the \Verb|\subsection| command. If you use the \Verb|\MakeUppercase| command to autocapitalize chapter titles, carefully check each title and its table of contents entry for compliance with University formatting requirements. You may wish to split chapters into separate .tex files for easier document management. You can use the \Verb|\include{}| command to add the chapters to your main .tex file. Click \underline{\href{https://www.overleaf.com/learn/latex/Management_in_a_large_project\#Inputting_and_including_files}{here}} to learn more.

If a chapter or section title contains math expressions or other symbols, the \Verb|hyperref| package may give a PDFDocEncoded warning when you compile your document. Use the \Verb|\texorpdfstring| command to edit the corresponding PDF bookmark text to resolve the warning. 
For example, the section title ``Lemurs Wrote $E=mc^2$ in Mud on the Riverbank'' should be coded
\begin{Verbatim}
 \section{Lemurs Wrote \texorpdfstring{$E=mc^2$}{E=mc2} in Mud on the Riverbank}   
\end{Verbatim}

\chapter{\MakeUppercase{Citations and Bibliography}} \label{ch:citebib}
LaTeX bibliography packages and citation management programs enable you to efficiently cite and format bibliographic references. You can manage bibliographic entries in a separate bibliography database file (*.bib) or you can embed the entries directly into the main .tex file.
